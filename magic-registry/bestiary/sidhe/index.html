The Account

The Sidhe are the old gods of Ireland gone under the hills, ageless and beautiful so long as the land is whole, and burned by the cold iron that unmakes it.

T

The learned men of Ireland wrote that the island had been taken five times before the Gaels came, and that the last to hold it were the Tuatha Dé Danann, a people of great skill and beauty who had come out of the north in a cloud. When the sons of Míl defeated them, the Tuatha Dé went into the hills. An old tale in the Book of Leinster tells how the Dagda shared out the mounds among them, and how the Mac Óc asked for the Brú on the Boyne for a day and a night and then kept it for ever. Those mounds are the síde, and the people in them have been called by the mounds’ name ever since.

Of their kinds the countryfolk name many, but the Registry counts these, the people of the hollow hills, as one. They are tall and fair and do not grow old. They feast, and war among themselves, and keep a court; and sometimes they take a mortal in, for love or for a game, and the mortal comes out after what seems a night to find a hundred years gone.

They are found in the old mounds: at the Brú on the Boyne, in Connacht, in the green hills with stones at their tops that no farmer will plough. They keep the great days of the year, Samhain and Beltane above all, when the doors of the hills stand open.

Of their bodies this much is certain: they are kept by the land, as a spring is kept by its hill. Where the ground is sweet and the woods are standing, they do not fade; where it is broken and spoiled, they sicken. Iron above all things they cannot bear. The Reverend Robert Kirk, minister of Aberfoyle, set down in 1691 that the Highland people put bread, the Bible or a piece of iron in the bed of a woman in labour, so that she would not be stolen away by them.

They are not to be crossed, nor thanked, nor followed into the hill. Courtesy is the whole of safety with them. A cut thorn or a ploughed mound has been paid for with a family’s luck, and the hill does not forget.

Body: humanoid, taller than human, with no decline of tissue over time. Source: the First Tier, received through the Grid from the land itself. Mechanism: the body is maintained by the living environment around it; where the land is healthy, they do not age; where it is spoiled, they weaken. Their vigour follows the year: lowest in deep winter, highest in spring. Weakness: worked iron, which burns the skin on contact and dulls the senses at a distance. Behaviour: withdrawn, territorial and exact about courtesy. Containment: none is possible or wise; the room observes them on their own ground. Hazard to the observer: moderate, and mostly to those who trespass or give offence.

Its Habits

The glass that shows a creature's inward parts is set in iron here. Where it passes over them their light goes out, and they turn away from it.The fluoroscope lens counts as iron. Under it, the luminescence dies and the skin blisters; the figure recoils away from the lens.

The hill keeps the reader's own season. In winter its people are dim and few, in spring they shine, in summer they are full, and in autumn they begin to fade.The room follows the season of the reader's hemisphere: dormant in winter, most active in spring, full in summer, waning in autumn.

To read them without wounding them, leave the glass aside. Every part it would show is also set down in words below.With focus on the specimen, the arrow keys move the lens; by touch, it is set down wherever a finger touches. With reduced motion, one still plate shows a single blistered patch.

Its Nearest Neighbour

The Fae. The Fae are also called the people of the hill, but the Fae have no flesh; they are the terms of the bargain, not the hands that make it. The Sidhe have bodies, and bleed.Both are the folk under the hill in folklore. The Sidhe are bodily and land-bound. The Fae are bodiless.
