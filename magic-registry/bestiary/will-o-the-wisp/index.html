The Account

The Will-o'-the-wisp is a pale light that hangs over bogs at night and moves off as a traveller follows, always just ahead, until the path is gone and the ground gives way.

A bodiless light of the First Tier, sustained by the Grid in waterlogged ground, which leads anyone who follows it away from what the ground holds.

T

There was a man called Will, the tellers of Shropshire say, a blacksmith so wicked that neither Heaven nor Hell would have him. The Devil gave him a single coal from the fire to warm himself, and he wanders the marshes with it still, leading travellers into the water. That is one story of the light. There are as many as there are counties with bogs in them: Jack with his lantern, Peg with hers, the corpse-candles of Wales that go before a funeral along the road it will take.

Of their kinds the Registry counts one, and fox-fire with it. Whatever the light is called, it does the same thing.

It is found on wet ground at night: fens, bogs, marshes, churchyards in low country. Milton made it an image of the tempter. In the ninth book of his poem, the serpent leads Eve to the tree like a wandering fire that misleads the night traveller into the pool. Alessandro Volta stirred the mud of Lake Maggiore in 1776 and lit the gas that came up, and the learned took the question of the marsh-lights to be settled. It has not been settled since.

Of its body this much is certain: it has none, and it gives no heat. It hangs over the pools and keeps its distance. Travellers who follow it find the ground going soft under them, and those who keep following do not come back. Tolkien’s travellers crossing the Dead Marshes saw candles in the pools, and faces under the water. They were told not to follow the lights.

It is not to be followed. A traveller lost on the moor at night should stop where the ground is firm and wait for daylight. The light will go on without him.

Body: none. A diffuse light, bluish or greenish, from a candle flame to a lantern in size, hanging a little above the surface. Source: the First Tier, concentrated where the Grid runs through standing water and peat. Mechanism: the light keeps a fixed distance ahead of an observer and moves away along unsafe ground; it withdraws further if pursued. The old explanation (marsh gas lit by itself) covers some sightings and not others. Function, as recorded: to keep trespassers from the bog's dead and its hoards. Containment: none needed; the light does not approach. Hazard to the observer: drowning, to anyone who follows.

Under the Screen

The flame

The light is cold. A hand held near it feels nothing, and a straw held in it does not burn.

No measurable heat.

The halo

Around the light is a faint ring of mist that moves with it, so that from a distance it looks like a lantern carried by someone unseen.

A halo of condensation accompanies the light.

The pool

Under the light, when the screen is put to it, the water shows what it keeps: an old track, a bronze blade, a face.

The fluoroscope reveals objects preserved in the peat beneath the light.

Its Habits

It keeps ahead of the reader's hand, and wherever it goes the bog's own note comes up out of the dark. Follow it to the edge of the case, and it lures a drawn light down into the pool. The reader's own hand is never slowed.The light stays a fixed distance ahead of the reader's hand and reveals the hidden note where it passes. At the margins a drawn decoy sinks into the pool. Real movement is never altered.

On a dark night, with the moon new or under the horizon, there are more lights and they burn brighter. By day there is only a shimmer over the pools.At night with the moon less than 25 percent lit or below the horizon, more lights appear, brighter. By day only a faint shimmer is shown.

The bog's note can be read whole by coming to it directly, without following the light.Keyboard focus on the note reveals it entire; by touch, a finger leads the light. With reduced motion the note is shown and the light is still.

Its Nearest Neighbour

The Kitsune. The fox's fires in the fields of Japan are lights of the same kind, and the Registry files them here. The fox itself is flesh, and the fire is only its lantern.Fox-fire is filed under the Will-o'-the-wisp; the Kitsune is a bodied creature.
