The Account

The Wraith is not the dead walking but a moment that a place has kept, which plays again for those who are there at the right hour, and has no one inside it.

A bodiless impression of a past event, stored in the stone and metal of a place by the earth's own current and played back to observers, with no person in it.

I

In February 1953 an apprentice heating engineer named Harry Martindale was up a ladder in the cellar of the Treasurer’s House in York when he heard a horn. A soldier in a helmet stepped out of the wall, and after him came a horse and a column of soldiers in the dress of Rome, walking two by two, tired and dirty. They were visible only from the knees up. In the middle of the cellar, where the floor had been dug down to an old Roman road, their feet could be seen. He fell off his ladder and hid in the corner until they had gone. He told the story the same way for the rest of his life.

Of their kinds the tellers name many: the grey lady on the stair, the drummer on the battlements, the coach that comes up the drive on one night of the year. The Registry counts as wraiths those that do the same thing every time and take no notice of the living. It counts them as one.

They are found in old houses, on battlefields and at the sites of violent deaths: places where stone and water have stood a long time. T. C. Lethbridge, an archaeologist who took the matter seriously, thought that the water in such places held a sort of field that could take an impression from strong feeling and give it back. Nigel Kneale made a play of the idea in 1972. A team of researchers in an old house find that its stones have recorded a scream, and that the recording has more beneath it.

Of their bodies this much is certain: there is no one in them. The soldiers in the cellar did not see the plumber. The grey lady does not see the guests. What walks is the record, and the record wears.

It is not to be spoken to, for it cannot answer. Nothing is to be feared from it but the cold, and the knowledge of what happened there.

Body: none. What is seen and heard is a repetition: the same figures, the same gestures, the same sounds, always in the same order. Source: the Second Tier, as the slow current of the earth through stone, iron and water, which takes an impression from a strong event and keeps it. Mechanism: at certain times and to certain observers the impression is released and plays through. It does not respond to the observer. It degrades with each playing. Signs: cold; a fixed route; figures at the wrong height for the present floor. Containment: unnecessary. Hazard to the observer: fright only.

Its Nearest Neighbour

The Succubus. The Succubus also comes in the night, and also from the deep, but it presses on a living sleeper. The Wraith presses on no one; it is only the room remembering.Both are bodiless and of the Second Tier. The Wraith is a place's recording with no one in it; the Succubus acts on a living sleeper.

Its Habits

Reading on tears the wire, and the moment jumps and stutters. Turning back a little steadies it, as a spool steadies when it is wound back.Moving down the passage breaks up the playback. Moving back up briefly steadies it.

The brighter the moon in the reader's sky, the more noise there is on the wire, and the harder the moment is to hear clearly.Noise in the playback increases with the moon's illuminated fraction.

Read on as far as is wished; the words are never torn, only the wire.The up arrow steadies the playback; by touch, a short swipe back up. With reduced motion the playback is clean.
