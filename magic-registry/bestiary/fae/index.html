The Fae

The Bargain That Keeps ItselfTerms, names and thresholds that act without a body

The Account

The Fae are the Good Folk, who have no bodies of their own but only the shapes they choose to wear, and whose every gift is a bargain and every bargain is kept to the letter.

A bodiless pattern of the First Tier, made of terms, names and thresholds, which enforces any agreement struck with it exactly as worded.

T

The country people of Britain and Ireland were careful not to name them. They called them the Good Folk, the Gentry, the Fair Family, the People of Peace, and never anything that might give offence. A name was a thing to be careful with, for what could be named could be called, and what could be called could be bound.

Of their kinds the tales name hundreds: brownies and pixies, the fair ladies at the well, the little men in green who dance in rings. The Registry counts them as one, for in every tale they keep the same law.

They are found at thresholds: at doorsteps and stiles, crossroads, the edges of woods, the green rings in the grass where the mushrooms come up. The great days are theirs, when the year turns, and the dusk of every day.

Of their bodies this much is certain: they have none. They wear what shape they please, and the shapes do not matter. What matters is the terms. A miller’s daughter must turn straw into gold, and a little man does it for her. The price is her necklace, then her ring, and then her first child, and the terms hold until she finds out his name. Thomas of Ercildoune kissed the queen of Elfland and served her seven years for it. He came home unable to tell a lie, because she had made him a gift of a tongue that could not.

They are not to be thanked, for thanks may be taken as a debt paid. Their food is not to be eaten, for whoever eats it stays. A gift from them is never to be accepted without asking what is owed for it.

Body: none. The figures seen are worn shapes, usually small or beautiful, and are not the thing itself. Source: the First Tier: the arithmetic of what is owed. Mechanism: an exchange, once struck, is carried out by the pattern without further act by either party. The terms are read literally. A true name gives the holder power over the named; a threshold crossed by invitation is crossed for good. Weakness: precise refusal; iron at thresholds; a name discovered. Containment: none; only careful wording. Hazard to the observer: grave for anyone who accepts a gift or eats their food.

Its Habits

Whatever words are taken up from the page, the lattice takes up too, and writes a copy of them in its own hand beside them, so that nothing is taken without a term.Selecting text draws the lattice to the selection and writes a visible enciphered echo of the passage beside it. Nothing the reader copies is altered.

At the old turning-days of the year, Beltane, Samhain, Imbolc and Lughnasadh, and at dusk on any day, the lattice is thicker and its writing spreads further.Within three days of 1 May, 1 November, 1 February or 1 August, and at local dusk, the lattice density and enciphering radius are greater.

To leave the words alone is to strike no bargain at all.Selection by keyboard (select all, then the shift key and the arrows to narrow it) or by a long touch works the same. With reduced motion the lattice is drawn still.

Its Nearest Neighbour

The Sidhe. The Sidhe are also called the people of the hill, but the Sidhe have bodies and bleed. The Fae are the terms, not the hands that sign them.Both are the folk under the hill in folklore. The Sidhe are bodily; the Fae are bodiless.
