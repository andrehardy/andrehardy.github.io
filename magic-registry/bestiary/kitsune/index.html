The Account

The Kitsune is a fox that has lived long enough to take any shape it likes, and gains a tail for every age of its wisdom, up to nine.

A Chimeric fox that changes shape at will, accumulates foresight from the First Tier over long life, and stores its accumulated age as additional tails, to a maximum of nine.

I

In the oldest book of wonders in Japan, the Nihon Ryōiki, a man of Mino province marries a beautiful woman he has met on a moor. She bears him a son, and one day the household dog frightens her into her true shape, a fox. The man will not let her go. “Come and sleep,” he tells her, and the book says this is why the fox is called kitsune. Whether or not that is the true root of the name, it is the true root of the creature: a fox that can be a woman, and that is loved.

Of its kinds the tellers count many: the white foxes that serve the rice god at Fushimi, the wild foxes of the fields, the wicked fox that came from India by way of China to bewitch an emperor. The Registry counts one, for they differ in temper and not in nature.

It is found at the edges of things: at shrine gates, on moors, at the borders between field and wood. On the last night of the year the foxes of the Kantō were said to gather at Ōji with their fires, and the farmers read the coming harvest by the number of the lights.

Of its body this much is certain: it grows. A fox that lives a hundred years gains a second tail, and with every age that follows another, until at nine tails it is a fox of a thousand years and is said to see across the land. With each tail comes more of the fox’s wisdom, and more of its power over shapes. It carries a pearl, the star ball, which is said to hold some part of its life; a fox that loses it will do anything to have it back.

It is not to be trapped, or slighted, or followed onto the moor. A fox repays kindness for generations and an insult for longer. Look for its shadow on a paper screen, or for the tail that slips out when it sleeps, and keep its pearl safe if it is ever left in human hands.

Body: vulpine, with fully voluntary shape-change, most often to a young woman or an old man. Source: the First Tier, accumulated slowly over a long life; age is power. Mechanism: each century (the common count) adds a tail; at nine tails the fox is near the limit of its kind. It carries a pearl or ball (hoshi no tama), said to hold part of its life, which must not be lost. Signs: a shadow or reflection that shows the fox; fox-fire lights at night; a tail that slips out in sleep or drink. Behaviour: varies from loyal to malicious; foxes in the service of the rice god are benevolent. Containment: none; the room observes. Hazard to the observer: low to grave, depending on the fox.

Under the Screen

The tails

Each tail is a store of years. Under the screen they show as fine, branching light, like frost on a window, and the oldest is the most branched.

Each tail is a store of accumulated age; older tails show more complex branching under the lens.

The pearl

The pearl at its feet is cloudy to the eye and bright to the screen, and something in it moves like breath.

The hoshi no tama is opaque to light and luminous to the fluoroscope; it pulses slowly.

The eyes

The eyes have the fox's slit, whatever face the fox is wearing.

Vertical pupils persist through every change of shape.

Its Habits

It will not open to a restless eye. Keep still, and one by one the tails unfold behind it, and the pearl brightens; stir, and the fox gathers itself back into one.Idle stillness unfurls one tail every 1.2 seconds, up to the ceiling. Any movement folds them back to one.

A fox gains its tails with age, and this one with acquaintance: for every separate day the reader has come to look at it, it will show one more, until it shows nine.The tail ceiling is one plus the number of distinct days the room has been seen, today included, to a maximum of nine. The count is kept with the reader and nowhere else.

Stillness is the whole of the matter, and a reader who does nothing at all will see the most.With no input the tails open by themselves; keyboard and touch readers see the same. With reduced motion the fox is shown in two plates: folded, and with all nine open.

Its Nearest Neighbour

The Lycanthrope. The wolf also changes, but the moon changes him, and every change breaks him. The fox chooses its shape, and every century makes it wiser.Lycanthrope change is forced and traumatic. Kitsune change is voluntary and cumulative.
