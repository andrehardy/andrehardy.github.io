The OathSummary

The Paladin is a sworn mortal held in the Vault's favour, so that every blow aimed true goes somehow astray for as long as the vow is kept.

A Paladin is a baseline human committed to a First Tier purpose, around whom the First Tier keeps a defensive field that makes harm aimed at the Paladin improbable.

The Account of the OathHow the power works

The Paladin swore, and the sky heard. That is the whole of the matter; the rest follows from it.

There is no fire in the Paladin’s hands and no strength beyond a soldier’s. What there is, is favour. The Vault, which disposes every likelihood under heaven, has taken this one mortal into its keeping for the sake of the vow, and so the world about the Paladin falls out a little kindly. The arrow meets a gust. The axe-head flies from a cracked haft. The lance that would have found the gap in the mail finds the boss of the shield instead, by a hand’s breadth, again and again, until the enemy begins to speak of miracles.

It is not a miracle. It is likelihood leaned upon, as the Wizard leans upon it, but the Paladin does no reckoning: the favour reckons for itself, and only against harm. It will not throw the enemy down and it will not open the gate. A Paladin who would win must win as any knight wins, by steel and nerve, with the favour standing behind like a squire who only ever parries.

The painted windows crowd other gifts into the Paladin’s hands, the healing touch above all. That gift is the Cleric’s. Where a knight both shields and heals, the Registry sees two arts in one coat, and files the knight under the shield.

And the vow cuts both ways. The favour holds while the purpose is kept, and whoever turns from it finds, at the next blow, that the luck has gone out of the world. Nor does it shelter anyone else: arrows that swerve from the Paladin land somewhere, and wise companions stand behind and never beside.

Being: a baseline human, committed to a purpose. Source: the First Tier. Access: probability shielding.

A Paladin has no unusual strength and works no spells. The First Tier, which governs probability, keeps a field around a person who is acting for one of its purposes, and inside that field harm aimed at the Paladin becomes unlikely to land. Arrows are caught by sudden gusts, blades break on hidden flaws and a charging horse stumbles. Each event is ordinary; their reliable arrival is not.

The Paladin does not control the field. It answers threats to the Paladin, not the Paladin's wishes, so it cannot be aimed, cannot be used to attack and does nothing while nothing threatens. The oath matters because the field depends on the purpose: a Paladin who abandons it is unprotected.

Fiction often gives the Paladin a healing touch. Healing belongs to the Cleric and the Sealed Broadcast; the Registry treats a healing knight as two practices in one person, and files the knight by the shield.

Limits: the field is defensive only and protects only the Paladin, so deflected harm can strike companions. It acts on discrete events, so slow harms such as hunger and disease pass through it. It ends when the purpose is abandoned.

The oath

Spoken once on a vigil night and kept every day after, it sets the terms on which the favour is held. Break them, and the mail of fortune falls away like a cloak unpinned.

The shield

Painted with the arms of a house, and the least of the Paladin's defences: the steel turns what it meets, and the favour turns what it never has to meet.

The Glass upon the TableTrial: bring down a glass

The glass stands, unless it holds a danger to the Paladin, and then a draught from nowhere knocks it over first.

Told to bring the glass down, the Paladin has no wonder to offer: the favour will not move a cup for a show, and a sworn knight will not pretend that it might. But let the wine be poisoned and the Paladin's hand go out to it, and somewhere a shutter swings in a gust it had no reason to catch, and the glass lies broken on the floor before the fingers close.

The field cannot be directed, so the Paladin has no unusual means of moving the glass. If the wine were poisoned and the Paladin were about to drink it, the First Tier would make an ordinary event likely, such as a draught from a window, that tips the glass before the Paladin can drink.

The Stranger in the ChairTrial: empty an occupied chair

The stranger stays seated, unless the stranger strikes at the Paladin, and then the blow comes back.

A stranger who only sits is no threat, and the favour has nothing to do. The Paladin may wait, but the vow forbids dragging a peaceable soul from a seat. Should the stranger raise a crossbow and loose, though, the bolt strikes a rivet of the Paladin's harness at the one angle in a thousand that sends it back, and takes the stranger in the shoulder, and the chair goes over.

The field acts only against threats to the Paladin. If the person does nothing, the Paladin has no means of moving them. If the person attacks, for example by shooting, the First Tier makes an improbable deflection likely; the missile rebounds, strikes the attacker and knocks them out of the chair.

The exemplar · Game

Master Chief (John-117)

Halo, 2001

Called “Spartan” in the work; filed here as the Paladin.

The Master Chief is the Registry's model Paladin, because what carries him alive through every lost battle is not his armour but his luck, and his own legend says so.

The Master Chief is a Paladin: a soldier sworn to a defensive purpose whose survival against overwhelming odds is explained by improbable good fortune rather than by strength or equipment.

The Master Chief is the Registry's model Paladin, because what carries him alive through every lost battle is not his armour but his luck, and his own legend says so.

He is armoured and made strong, and that would be enough for a good soldier; it is not enough for what he survives. Again and again he is the one who walks out of the fall of a world, the wreck of a ship or the breaking of a ring, when stronger soldiers and whole fleets do not. His story says it plainly. He was picked out as a boy because a tossed coin fell his way, and the companion who rides in his armour chose him, out of all the others, for his luck. The Registry takes the legend at its word: a soldier sworn to defend humankind, on whom the odds keep falling kindly, is a Paladin in powered plate.

The Mandalorian keeps a creed as a knight keeps a vow. He never shows his face, he answers every hard choice with the same few words, and he takes up a foundling's cause and holds to it through every bounty that tempts him to let it go. His armour turns the bolts of a whole street of hired guns, and he walks out from under their fire unhurt.
