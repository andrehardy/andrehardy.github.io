The PropositionSummary

The Wizard is an ordinary mortal who has learned the arithmetic of the sky, and works wonders by asking the Vault to make the unlikely likely rather than by moving what is heavy.

A Wizard is a baseline human who, through study alone, learns to address the First Tier in its own formulae, asking it to make one chosen outcome more probable at one place and time.

The AccountHow the power works

The Wizard was born with nothing. No old blood stirs in those veins, no bargain was struck below, no mushroom or vine opened the door. There are only the desk, the candle and the sky’s long tables, learned figure by figure over the better part of a life, until at last the Vault will answer.

What the tables teach is that the sky does not push; it disposes. Every hour holds a great many morrows, and the Vault chooses among them by likelihood, as a river chooses its course by the lie of the land. The Wizard learns to read that lie, and then to ask, in the sky’s own figures, that it be altered by a hair: a word spoken at the right hour, a staff set down on the right stone, and the Vault, so asked, leans. What follows looks like wonder. It is arithmetic carried through to its end.

So the Wizard throws no fire. Where the Sorcerer hurls flame from the open palm, the Wizard waits for the marsh to breathe out its foul air and the storm to strike in the same instant, having arranged that both should come together. The flame is the same; the art is altogether different, and the Registry files the two apart, whatever their robes and wands may say.

This is why so many who are called wizards are not. A child who shatters a window in a fit of temper was born to power, and is a Sorcerer however long the beard grows afterwards. The true Wizard is known by a single sign: unlearn every page, and an ordinary mortal is all that remains, while a Sorcerer stripped of every lesson would still burn.

The art keeps a hard account. It is slow, and a working that must wait upon circumstance is of no use once the blade is already falling. It is heavy, for great sums fill the mind until the body sickens under them. And it is blind to whatever keeps no steady pattern: the changeling who wears a new face every hour, and the heart that chooses the unlikely thing for love.

Being: a baseline human, with no inherited capacity. Source: the First Tier. Access: calculation.

A Wizard's power is entirely learned, and none of it is the Wizard's own. The First Tier governs probability. A Wizard studies the formulae that describe it until they can work out exactly which small, ordinary event, at which place and moment, would produce the result they want. They then speak the formula through the staff, aimed at that place, and the First Tier makes that event more likely until it happens. The Wizard supplies the calculation and the aim; the First Tier supplies the change. Staffs, spellbooks and incantations are instruments of accuracy, not stores of power.

Because the First Tier does not act on matter, a Wizard never produces force directly. Every visible effect is an ordinary physical event made probable at the chosen time: a fireball is leaking marsh gas and a lightning strike made to arrive together, not a flame thrown from the hand. A character who produces force from the body, especially as an untrained child, is a Sorcerer, whatever the story calls them.

Limits: results need time and suitable circumstances, so a Wizard is weakest in sudden physical danger. Large calculations exhaust the practitioner, and errors go wrong unpredictably. Bodies that change their own pattern (Tier III Alpha) cannot be tracked by calculation.

The InstrumentsTools

The staff

Not a lever but a lightning rod: a length of seasoned wood tipped with crystal, raised to the Vault and set down on the one place where the reckoning says the change must fall, so that the sky's answer comes to ground there and nowhere else.

A directing instrument. Its crystal tip marks the exact point where the requested change is to happen and carries the request to the First Tier, as a lightning rod gives a strike one place to land. It stores no power.

The priceCosts

  1. Years. No Wizard is born; every one is made slowly, at a desk, by candlelight, and many die before the arithmetic will answer them.

    Training takes years and most students never become proficient. There is no shortcut, because nothing is inherited.

  1. The changeling. A creature whose flesh changes at will leaves no steady mark in the sky's ledger, and slips out of every reckoning made to find it.

    Calculation works on patterns. Bodies that rewrite their own pattern (Tier III Alpha) change faster than they can be predicted, so a Wizard cannot track them.

The Witch

Legend gave this quarrel two faces, Merlin and Morgan le Fay, and in doing so proved that one word, magic, had been stretched over two arts. He read the sky and arranged what would happen; she worked flesh and earth and changed what was. He was never in the room when she came for him, and she was never where his reckoning looked. The Registry files Morgan herself as an Enchantress, but the war the legend tells is the war of these two arts, and neither won it: Camelot fell between them.

The Wizard works on likelihood (the First Tier); the Witch works on matter and chemistry (the Second). Neither can target the other well. A Wizard's predictions lose track of a body that changes, and a Witch cannot catch someone whose every path has been made unlikely to cross hers. The legend of Merlin and Morgan le Fay is the classic case, although the Registry files Morgan as an Enchantress rather than a Witch.

The Sorcerer

The Sorcerer has by birth what the Wizard earns by study, and spends it without counting. One builds a working stone by stone; the other breaks from the ground like a spring. The world confuses them because both may carry a wand.

The Glass upon the TableTrial: bring down a glass

The glass falls, and nothing touched it but the floor.

The Wizard does not reach for the glass. Having reckoned the room (the age of its boards, the beam that carries the table's near leg, the fault already running through the grain) the Wizard shifts weight and sets the staff down once, on one exact place. The old timber settles by a finger's breadth. The table leans, and the glass goes over as though the house itself had sighed.

No force crosses the room. The Wizard finds a weakness that already exists, a cracked board over the beam that supports the table, and adds the smallest push that makes it give: a shift of weight and a tap of the staff at the right spot. The floor sinks slightly, the table tilts and the glass slides off. The skill is in the calculation, not in the push.

The Stranger in the ChairTrial: empty an occupied chair

The stranger leaves of their own accord, believing it was their own idea.

The Wizard lays no hand on the stranger and speaks no charm at them. Instead a run of small chances is gathered and set in order: a bird strikes the window, the stranger turns to look, and in that moment a messenger arrives with word that cannot wait. Each link is unremarkable, and each was asked of the Vault; the craft lies in choosing them and setting them in order. The stranger rises and goes, and will tell it afterwards as a day of ill luck.

The First Tier tilts the odds; the bird strikes, the message comes, and leaving becomes the person's own sensible decision. The method works only through circumstances that the person responds to freely.

Proud, he tries a working beyond his learning, to call a spirit back from the dead, and tears the world open, and a shadow comes through and hunts him. He ends his flight only when he learns its name, and it is his own. The Registry files him with the Wizards: power as exact knowledge, and the price of knowledge rushed.

  • Magic in Earthsea works through knowledge of a thing's true name in the Old Speech.
  • Ged studies at the school for wizards on Roke, where the Master Namer teaches the true names of things.
  • He ends the shadow he released only by learning its name and speaking it: Ged.
