The Burden of the SongSummary

The Bard has learned that every thing, a goblet, a wall, a heart, has a note of its own, and that the right note, sung or struck, will shake it until it gives way.

A Bard is an ordinary person who uses precisely produced sound to act on objects through resonance and on people through the body's responses to pitch, rhythm and very low frequencies.

The Account of the NoteHow the power works

Every thing in the world has a note of its own. Strike a goblet with a fingernail and it sings it; a bell sings it louder, and a stone wall, if it could be struck hard enough, would sing it too. The Bard is one who has spent a lifetime learning to hear those notes, and to sing them back.

Sung back, they do work. A goblet held at its own pitch trembles, then rings, then flies apart. A doorway can be made to shed its mortar. And people have notes as well, though not in their glass: the rhythm that steadies a frightened column, the interval that makes a hall weep, the deep thrum below hearing that turns the stomach and fills a brave man with a terror he cannot name.

The Bard is reckoned a wanderer, a teller of tales and a charmer at the table, and is all of these. But the lute is tuned by ear to whatever stands across the room, and the drum at the Bard’s side is played too low for a feast. The craft is in the measure: the right note, at the right strength, held for the right count.

It is a learned art, and a physical one. Nothing comes from the sky and nothing is borrowed; the Bard was born like anyone else and simply listened harder. That is also its weakness. A note must be heard, and wax in the ears, a gale or a shuttered window will stop it. It strikes every ear in reach, friend and foe alike. And it spends the hearing of the one who makes it, so that most old Bards play by memory and by touch.

Being: a baseline human. Source: the Second Tier, through vibration. Access: Acoustics.

The Second Tier governs mass, pressure and vibration, and every physical thing vibrates most easily at certain pitches, its resonant frequencies. A Bard is trained to find those pitches and produce them precisely, with an instrument or the voice. Sustained at the right pitch and volume, the sound drives an object to vibrate until it fails: glass cracks, mortar loosens, a string snaps.

People respond to sound as well. Rhythm and interval change mood, attention and courage, which is why a march steadies a column and a lullaby quiets a room. Very low frequencies act directly on the inner ear and the eye, producing dizziness, blurred vision, nausea and fear with no apparent cause. A skilled Bard combines these to frighten, calm, rally or disorient.

None of this requires belief or consent. The effect is in the sound, so it can be taught, written down and repeated by anyone with the training.

Limits: the effect needs a clear path to the target and weakens with distance, walls and noise. Sound reaches everyone in range, not only the target. Deafened listeners and reanimated bodies are unaffected except by force strong enough to break them. Long practice damages the Bard's hearing.

Before the TrialsTrial results

The Glass upon the TableTrial: bring down a glass

The glass shatters where it stands, though no one has touched it.

The Bard flicks the rim of a cup at the near end of the table, listens, and nods. Then a single note is sung, softly at first, drawing up and up until it finds the pitch that the glass across the room has been singing to itself all along. The glass answers, and rings, and cannot stop ringing, and flies apart in a spray of wine.

The Bard finds the resonant frequency of the glass and produces it with the voice, sustained and growing louder. The glass vibrates with increasing force until it breaks, with no physical contact.

Arrow, 2015

Laurel Lance is the Registry's model Bard, because she was born with no gift at all and fights with a cry: a voice made into a weapon by a made thing at her throat.

She is a lawyer with no strange blood and no master below, who took up her dead sister's mantle and learned to fight in the streets. What she brings to the fight is her voice. A craftsman's collar at her throat takes her scream and makes it into a blast that bursts windows and knocks grown men off their feet. It is a made thing, and a learned skill, and it belongs entirely to the world of physical things. And it has the Bard's weakness: those who fight beside her stop their ears with wax made for the purpose, because the cry will strike any ear in reach. The Registry knows no plainer picture of the Bard in battle.

These shatter glass and knock adults off their feet. Laurel has no inherited ability; the effect is acoustics produced through a made instrument. Her allies wear earplugs designed to block the cry, which shows that the effect depends on being heard. That is the Bard's access, Acoustics.

In the book his weapon is the body, trained in an old order's secret way of fighting; the film makes it sound. The Registry ranks him second, and for this telling only, because the Paul of the books is not a Bard at all: his greater gift is the sight that the spice opens, and it is filed elsewhere.

Orpheus

Metamorphoses, AD 8

Called “the Thracian bard” in the work; filed here as the Bard.

Orpheus is filed among the Bards because his song moved trees and stones, and brought the lords of the dead to tears.

Orpheus is filed as a Bard because his effects on matter, beasts and listeners are produced by music alone.

Orpheus is filed as a Bard because his effects on matter, beasts and listeners are produced by music alone.

Orpheus is filed among the Bards because his song moved trees and stones, and brought the lords of the dead to tears.

When he played, the trees came down from the hills to stand around him, and the beasts lay quiet. When his bride died of a snake's bite, he went down to the house of the dead and sang before its king and queen; the shades wept, the wheel of torment stood still, and his wife was given back on one condition, that he not look behind him on the road up. He looked. Sailing with the heroes for the golden fleece, he played louder and sweeter than the Sirens and kept the crew at the oars. Even when he was torn apart, his head and his lyre went down the river still sounding. The Registry files him as a Bard of the oldest kind.

In Ovid's Metamorphoses (books 10 and 11), Orpheus draws trees, beasts and stones to him by playing the lyre. After his wife Eurydice dies of a snakebite, he descends to the underworld and sings before its rulers; the punished shades pause and the Furies weep, and Eurydice is released on condition that he not look back, which he does. In the Argonautica of Apollonius of Rhodes, he plays to drown out the song of the Sirens so that the Argonauts row past them. Effects on matter and on the body's responses produced by sound alone are the Bard's access, Acoustics.

Väinämöinen

Kalevala, 1849

Called “the eternal sage” in the work; filed here as the Bard.

Väinämöinen is filed among the Bards because he sings his rival into a bog, and plays a harp of fish-bone that brings every creature to listen and every listener to tears.

Väinämöinen is filed as a Bard because his power over matter and over listeners is exercised entirely through song and the kantele.

Väinämöinen is filed as a Bard because his power over matter and over listeners is exercised entirely through song and the kantele.

Väinämöinen is filed among the Bards because he sings his rival into a bog, and plays a harp of fish-bone that brings every creature to listen and every listener to tears.

He is the oldest of singers, born already old. When a brash young man challenges him to a contest of songs, he sings until the ground takes his challenger to the waist, then to the chest, then to the chin, and lets him go only when the young man promises his own sister. Later, out of the jaw of a great pike, he makes a harp, and when he plays it every creature of air, forest and water comes to hear; the listeners weep, and he weeps too, and his tears roll into the sea and become pearls. The Registry files him as a Bard: a voice that moves the earth itself.

In the Kalevala, compiled by Elias Lönnrot from Finnish and Karelian oral poetry, Väinämöinen is an aged singer and seer. In the third poem, the young Joukahainen challenges him to a singing contest; Väinämöinen sings him into a swamp and releases him only after he promises his sister Aino in marriage. In the fortieth and forty-first poems, he makes a kantele from the jawbone of a giant pike, and his playing draws every animal to listen and moves all who hear it, and himself, to tears. Effects on matter and on listeners produced by sound are the Bard's access, Acoustics.
