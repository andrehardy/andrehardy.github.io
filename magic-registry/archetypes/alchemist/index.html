1.What the Work IsSummary

The Alchemist was given nothing, by sky, deep or blood, and set out to take it all the same, with fire, glass and a notebook of failures, so that whatever the Alchemist can do was found out by trial and can be done again by anyone who reads the notes.

An Alchemist is an ordinary person with no source of power who imitates the effects of others by experimental chemistry and metallurgy, producing crude but repeatable results.

By the stars alone

2.The Account of the WorkHow the power works

The Alchemist is what becomes of an ordinary person who will not accept that power is something one is given. Neither sky nor deep has spoken to the Alchemist; no gift runs in the blood, and no patron waits at the crossroads. There is a cellar, a furnace, a rack of glass, and a notebook.

From these the Alchemist sets out to take what others were handed. If the Shaman opens the walls of the mind with a root, the Alchemist will distil something that does the same out of salts and spirits, and drink it, and write down what happened. If the deep can split stone, the Alchemist will find the powder that splits it too. Most of it fails. A hundred vessels crack for every one that answers, and the failures are struck through in the notebook but never torn out, because they are part of the knowledge.

The successes are cruder than the things they imitate, and far more dangerous to the one who makes them. Quicksilver shakes the hands, fumes scar the lungs, and laboratories have a way of ending in fire. But they have one virtue that no gift has: they can be done again. Whatever the Alchemist learns can be written down and taught, and works for anyone who follows the recipe exactly.

The old goals were gold from lead and life without end, and nobody has reached them. The Registry is not interested in that. It files the Alchemist apart from every other practitioner for a different reason: the Alchemist is the only one whose power owes nothing to anyone.

What the Alchemist cannot do is touch what has no weight. Chance, foresight and the Sealed Light cannot be poured into a flask, and they are beyond the reach of a craft that believes only in what it can measure.

Being: a baseline human. Source: none; the Unaligned. Access: Experiment.

The Alchemist has no source. There is no inherited capacity, no access to either tier and no contract. What the Alchemist has is method: careful experiment with substances, heat and metals, recorded so that it can be repeated. The aim, historically and in fiction, is to reproduce what the gifted can do by other means.

Two lines of work dominate. In the first, the Alchemist synthesises compounds that imitate the Keys of Ingestion and opens perception roughly, without the natural keys. In the second, the Alchemist refines heavy metals and reactive substances to imitate the force of the Second Tier: explosive mixtures, corrosives, anaesthetic vapours and the like. The legendary goals, gold from lead and the elixir of life, belong to the same project of taking by method what nature withheld.

Everything an Alchemist achieves is chemistry and physics, so it can be taught, written down and repeated by anyone with the procedure and the materials. That is the Alchemist's strength and the reason the Registry files the Alchemist with no source at all.

Limits: results are slow, crude and dangerous to the maker. Effects are limited to the material prepared. Nothing without substance, such as probability, the First Tier's record or the Sealed Broadcast, can be reached by experiment.

Glass, blown to the Alchemist's own drawing, with a beak that drips into the receiving flask all night. What went in as a stinking mash comes out as one clear drop.

There is a flash, a crack like a musket and a cloud of stinking yellow smoke, and when it clears the glass is on the floor and the tablecloth is on fire. It is loud, and it is messy, and it works every time.

He was a wandering physician of the German lands who quarrelled with every faculty that housed him, and taught from what the furnace showed him rather than from the old authorities. He set alchemy among the four pillars of the physician's art, and turned it from gold toward remedies drawn out of mercury, antimony and the other metals. Charged with poisoning his patients, he answered that all things are poison and nothing is without poison; the dose alone decides. The Registry files him as an Alchemist for that answer: a power found in the measure, not in the sky or the blood.

Paracelsus (Theophrastus von Hohenheim, 1493 or 1494 to 1541) was a Swiss-German physician who argued that alchemy should serve medicine rather than the making of gold, and who used mineral and metallic preparations as remedies. In the Seven Defences, written in 1538, he answered the charge that his remedies were poisons with the principle that the dose alone determines whether a substance is a poison. In Paragranum he named alchemy as one of the four pillars of medicine. Effects obtained from prepared substances, tested by trial and set by measure, are the Alchemist's access, Experiment.
