The Account of the FireHow the power works

The Sorcerer is the one the old stories mean when they speak of magic, and the one they most often misname. There is no book behind the Sorcerer, no pot, no bargain struck at a crossroads. There is only the blood, and something in it that was there before the first word was learned.

It wakes early, and seldom gently. A child is frightened, or furious, and the windows burst; a girl is cornered, and the man who cornered her is flung against the wall. After that it can be trained, as a horse is trained, but never quite broken. The force comes out of the Sorcerer’s own body, as lightning from the fingers, as a blow that falls without a hand, and it goes where the Sorcerer’s will points it.

The cost is paid in the same coin. Blood runs from the nose after a great working, the knees give way, and the Sorcerer may sleep like the dead for a day. Over a lifetime the strain tells on the mind as well as the body. The chronicles are full of kings with fire in their veins who began as wonders and ended in madness, and the Registry thinks the two facts belong together.

Much that the world calls wizardry is this. Whenever a story divides people into those born with the gift and those born without it, whenever power bursts out of a child who has never opened a book, the Registry looks past the robe and the wand and files a Sorcerer. The Wizard learns; the Sorcerer is born. A wand in the hand changes nothing.

What the Sorcerer cannot do is the subtle thing. The fire can break any door, but it cannot foretell which door will open, or mend what it has broken, or find a foe who is not in sight. It is strength, only strength, and it is paid for in flesh.

Being: Tier III, usually Gamma, sometimes Alpha. Source: none outside the body; the Unaligned. Access: Inherited capacity.

A Sorcerer is born able to generate force. The Tier III body, often human in appearance, carries an inherited capacity that the ordinary human body lacks: the nervous system can produce large electrical discharges, and can push or pull matter at a distance. No study, preparation, ritual or contract is involved. The capacity usually wakes in childhood or adolescence, often in a moment of fear or anger.

This is the power fiction most often calls magic, and the Registry files much of fiction's magic here. Any practitioner whose ability is inherited, who can act without learning and without tools, and who is born into a world divided between those who have the power and those who do not, is a Sorcerer, whatever the work calls them.

The cost is paid by the body. Heavy use causes bleeding, exhaustion and collapse, and long use is associated with instability and, in the worst cases, madness. The capacity responds to emotion and can discharge without intent.

Limits: the power is force, not knowledge or precision; a Sorcerer cannot calculate chance, foresee events or affect what cannot be perceived. Every use costs the Sorcerer's own body. It cannot be given up, lent or taught.

The hand

Open, or clenched, or flung out toward the thing that must break. It is not a tool so much as a direction; the force goes where the hand has pointed it.

The wand

The world puts one into every Sorcerer's hand, and it is a fine thing of holly or yew. It steadies the aim and calms the nerves. It has never yet made a Sorcerer, and a Sorcerer who loses it is not one whit the weaker.

Kindred FiresCompared with

The Wizard

The Wizard earns by study what the Sorcerer was given at birth, and spends it by the grain where the Sorcerer spends it by the bucket. One builds a working stone by stone and checks every sum; the other breaks out of the ground like a spring and cannot be stopped. Both may carry a wand, and that is why the world confuses them, and why the Registry sorts by where the power came from and not by what was in the hand.

The Wizard has no innate power and works by calculation through the First Tier, with precision and little force. The Sorcerer produces raw force from the body, with no study and little precision. A wand indicates neither: what separates them is whether the power was learned or inherited.

The Warlock

The Sorcerer was born with power and the Warlock bought it, and that is the whole of the difference, but it is everything. The Sorcerer's gift cannot be taken away, and neither can its cost. The Warlock's can be withdrawn, and its price was agreed in advance.
