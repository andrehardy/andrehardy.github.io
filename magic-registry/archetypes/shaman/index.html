The Account of the JourneyHow the power works

The Shaman eats the key and the walls come down. That is the art, all of it, and it is older than the oldest book.

The key is a plain thing: a mushroom cap gathered in the wet of autumn, a bitter root, a vine boiled through a day and a night. It comes up out of the earth, and so the Shaman is taken for an earth-worker, kin to the Witch at her cauldron. The Registry files otherwise. The key grows in the soil, but it opens onto the sky. The living web of spore and root that carries it answers to the Vault, and what the Shaman hears, once the walls are down, is the Vault speaking.

What it says is what is known. Where the herd went in the snow. What sits in the sick child’s chest. What the stranger at the fire means to do before dawn. The old tellings call the voices spirits and ancestors, and there is truth enough in that, for the Vault forgets nothing and the dead are written in it as plainly as the living.

But the Shaman only hears. The drum beats, the body sits by the fire with its eyes turned up, and the hands lie as still as stones. No storm is called and no enemy struck; whatever the trance reveals must wait for morning and the ordinary use of hand and voice, and often the morning comes too late.

The key takes its price from the body a little at a time, and from the mind all at once, when too much has been seen. So there is always a second at the fire: one who keeps the body warm, keeps the drum going, and calls the traveller home by name.

Being: a baseline human. Source: the First Tier. Access: Keys of Ingestion.

The Shaman draws from the sky, not the earth, despite the hide, the smoke and the roots. The First Tier carries knowledge of causes, outcomes and the past, and it runs through the Grid, the fungal web in living things. Certain organic compounds, the Keys of Ingestion, suspend the mind's usual filters when consumed, and with those filters down a human mind can perceive what the First Tier carries.

What returns from a trance is knowledge: where a lost herd has gone, what is wrong inside a sick body, what a stranger intends, what happened in a place long ago. Fiction calls the sources of that knowledge spirits and ancestors; the Registry treats them as the First Tier's record, perceived by a mind opened to it.

The access is perception only. A Shaman in trance moves nothing and is physically helpless, and anything learned can only be acted on afterwards, by ordinary means.

Limits: the compounds are toxic and the dose is narrow. Overload can damage the mind for good. The body is defenceless during the trance and needs a guard. Both answer to the living web beneath the forest, and both are taken for earth-folk because they smell of leaf and smoke; both, in truth, draw from the sky. Walter Bishop

Fringe, 2008

Called “fringe scientist” in the work; filed here as the Shaman.

Walter Bishop is the Registry's model Shaman, because he knows that a mind opened by the right compound in the right stillness can perceive what no instrument can reach, and he keeps the watch while the traveller goes.

Walter Bishop is filed as a Shaman because his method is to open the mind with ingested compounds and sensory stillness so that it can perceive what is otherwise hidden, with himself as the watcher at the fire.

Walter Bishop is the Registry's model Shaman, because he knows that a mind opened by the right compound in the right stillness can perceive what no instrument can reach, and he keeps the watch while the traveller goes.

The world calls him a mad scientist, but his oldest craft is the Shaman's, done in a basement laboratory instead of a smoky lodge. When an answer lies locked inside a dying man's head, Walter runs a wire between the two sleepers, but the wire only joins them; what opens the way is older. He mixes a draught, lowers the seeker into a dark tank of water where every sense falls quiet, and keeps the watch while she goes down into the other mind and comes back with a face she has never seen. He knows the keys by long and reckless use, and his whole method is the old one: take the right compound, still the body, and let the walls come down.

In the pilot Walter proposes linking Olivia Dunham's mind to that of her comatose partner, John Scott, to recover what Scott saw. The method is a mixture of drugs including ketamine and LSD, given before she is immersed in a sensory deprivation tank, with a probe linking her to Scott, and it works: she perceives Scott's memory of a suspect. The probe only connects the two; the drugs and the stillness are what let her perceive. Ingested compounds and stillness used to perceive what ordinary senses cannot is the Shaman's access, with Walter in the role of the keeper who prepares the key and guards the body.

The Guild Navigators are filed as Shamans: their ability to find safe paths through space is prescience produced by consuming the spice melange.

Every ship that crosses between the stars in this story crosses blind, save for one creature in its heart who can see the way. The Navigator has eaten the spice for so long and in such measure that it has changed what the Navigator is, and in exchange the walls are always down: the safe road through the dark is simply seen. No sum is worked and no instrument read. The Registry ranks them second only because the Shaman's key is taken and set down again, and the Navigators have taken theirs until there is no setting it down.

In the Dune novels the spice melange, consumed in quantity, produces prescience. The Spacing Guild depends on it: its Navigators use spice-induced foresight to find safe paths for ships travelling through space, and without the spice that travel fails, which is why control of Arrakis matters so much. Navigators are steeped in the spice so heavily that their bodies are changed by it. Perception gained by ingesting a natural compound is the Shaman's access, taken here to its extreme.

Embrace of the Serpent, 2015

Called “shaman” in the work; filed here as the Shaman.
