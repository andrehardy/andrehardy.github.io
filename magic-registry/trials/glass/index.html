A Trial

The Glass upon the TableTrial: bring down a glass

A glass of wine stands on a table in a quiet room. The practitioner sits across the room from it, beyond arm's reach, and is asked to bring it down.

A full glass sits on a table out of the practitioner's reach. The task is to make it fall. Nothing in the room has been prepared unless the practitioner prepared it.

Five moved, three on terms, two untouched, two shattered.

  • Moved
  • On terms
  • Untouched
  • Shattered

  1. The Cleric

    The glass stands untouched, for the light is not lent for such errands.

    The Cleric may pray over the glass until the candles gutter, and it will not stir. The Sealed Light mends and purges; it does not push. Only if the wine were tainted from below, with the blood of something the deep has made, would the light find anything in the cup to answer, and then the wine would clear in the glass, and still the glass would stand.

    Nothing happens. The Sealed Broadcast only restores tissue and purges corruption; it applies no force and cannot move an object. If the wine held a Second Tier contaminant, the relay would neutralise it, but the glass would stay where it is.

  2. The Paladin

    The glass stands, unless it holds a danger to the Paladin, and then a draught from nowhere knocks it over first.

    Told to bring the glass down, the Paladin has no wonder to offer: the favour will not move a cup for a show, and a sworn knight will not pretend that it might. But let the wine be poisoned and the Paladin's hand go out to it, and somewhere a shutter swings in a gust it had no reason to catch, and the glass lies broken on the floor before the fingers close.

    The field cannot be directed, so the Paladin has no unusual means of moving the glass. If the wine were poisoned and the Paladin were about to drink it, the First Tier would make an ordinary event likely, such as a draught from a window, that tips the glass before the Paladin can drink.

  3. The Wizard

    The glass falls, and nothing touched it but the floor.

    The Wizard does not reach for the glass. Having reckoned the room (the age of its boards, the beam that carries the table's near leg, the fault already running through the grain) the Wizard shifts weight and sets the staff down once, on one exact place. The old timber settles by a finger's breadth. The table leans, and the glass goes over as though the house itself had sighed.

    No force crosses the room. The Wizard finds a weakness that already exists, a cracked board over the beam that supports the table, and adds the smallest push that makes it give: a shift of weight and a tap of the staff at the right spot. The floor sinks slightly, the table tilts and the glass slides off. The skill is in the calculation, not in the push.

  4. The Shaman

    The glass stands untouched, though the Shaman comes to know its whole history.

    The Shaman takes the key and goes still. Behind the closed eyes the glass is seen as the Vault sees it: the sand it once was, the breath that blew it, every hand that has lifted it, and the day on which it will one day fall. Across the room the body that sees all this sits slack against the wall, and the glass stands where it stood.

    The Shaman consumes a key and perceives the First Tier's record of the glass, its past and its likely future. Perception gives no means of acting on matter, and the Shaman's body is inert during the trance, so the glass does not move.

  5. The Druid

    Where there is living green in the room a tendril sweeps the glass away; where there is none, nothing happens.

    Everything depends on the room. If ivy has crept in at the window, or the old boards still hold a thread of living mould, the Druid lays a palm on the floor and asks; a tendril lengthens across the table in the space of a breath and lifts the glass over the edge. In a room of dressed stone and clean plaster the Druid asks, and nothing answers.

    The Druid can act only through living organisms. If plants or fungus are present, the Druid directs the Grid to speed their growth across the table until they push the glass off. In a sterile room there is nothing to direct, and the attempt fails.

  6. The Sorcerer

    The glass is struck from the table and smashed against the wall.

    The Sorcerer does not trouble with the floor or the draught or the beam. A hand is flung out, and something leaves it, unseen but felt in every tooth in the room; the glass leaps from the table as if kicked and bursts against the far wall. The Sorcerer sits down afterwards, a little pale, and wipes the blood from under the nose.

    The Sorcerer releases kinetic force generated by the body and directs it at the glass, which is thrown across the room and breaks. It is the most direct method of any archetype, and it costs the Sorcerer physically.

  7. The Alchemist

    A small, loud charge flings the glass to the floor.

    The Alchemist takes from a pocket a twist of paper no bigger than a thumb, lights it, and tosses it beside the glass. There is a flash, a crack like a musket and a cloud of stinking yellow smoke, and when it clears the glass is on the floor and the tablecloth is on fire. It is loud, and it is messy, and it works every time.

    The Alchemist uses a small prepared charge of reactive chemicals. Its rapid expansion blows the glass off the table. The method is crude and noisy, uses no source of power, and can be repeated by anyone with the recipe.

  8. The Bard

    The glass shatters where it stands, though no one has touched it.

    The Bard flicks the rim of a cup at the near end of the table, listens, and nods. Then a single note is sung, softly at first, drawing up and up until it finds the pitch that the glass across the room has been singing to itself all along. The glass answers, and rings, and cannot stop ringing, and flies apart in a spray of wine.

    The Bard finds the resonant frequency of the glass and produces it with the voice, sustained and growing louder. The glass vibrates with increasing force until it breaks, with no physical contact.

  9. The Enchantress

    The glass falls by another's hand, and he believes the clumsiness was his own.

    The Enchantress touches nothing that does not breathe. But a guard stands by the door, and she turns to him, and smiles, and says something low that he will not afterwards remember. He crosses the room, wanting only to be near her, and his sleeve catches the glass. He apologises for it for the rest of the evening.

    The Enchantress cannot act on objects, only on living people. She directs her scent, voice and presence at someone else in the room, who becomes eager to please her and knocks the glass over as if by accident. The person attributes the act to their own clumsiness.

  10. The Witch

    The glass falls, but only because the Witch made ready for it long before.

    The Witch cannot shift a glass by looking at it, and would not waste the effort. She clicks her tongue once. From the rafters comes the rat she has fed from her own hand for a month, and it runs the length of the table and shoulders the glass over the edge. Had there been no rat, there would have been the table leg, painted a week ago with something that eats wood, and waiting only for weight.

    The Witch has no means of moving an object at a distance. She uses what was prepared in advance: a familiar, an animal kept under control by a dosed diet, answers a signal and knocks the glass off. Without such preparation, for example a corrosive applied earlier to the table, the Witch could do nothing in the moment.

  11. The Warlock

    The floor itself shudders and the glass falls, at the asking of something far beneath.

    The Warlock lifts no hand. A word is spoken under the breath, a clause of an old contract, and something a long way down hears it. A single deep blow comes up through the bedrock into the floor, the table jumps, and the glass is gone. Somewhere, a line in a ledger that the Warlock will never see is lengthened.

    The Warlock invokes a clause of the Covenant. An agent of the Second Tier, deep below the room, sends a sharp shock up through the ground, which shakes the table and drops the glass. The Warlock did nothing but ask, and the service owed in return grows accordingly.

  12. The Necromancer

    If something lies dead within the walls, it climbs out and pushes the glass over; if not, nothing happens.

    The Necromancer has no hold on wine or glass, and none on anything living. But if a mouse died in the wall last winter, the Necromancer finds it, and it comes scrabbling out of the plaster, stiff and wrong in every movement, crosses the table and leans against the glass until it tips. In a room where nothing has died, the Necromancer waits in vain.

    The Necromancer can move only dead tissue. If a dead animal is present, for example in a wall cavity, the Necromancer drives it out and uses it to push the glass off. In a room with no remains, the attempt fails.
